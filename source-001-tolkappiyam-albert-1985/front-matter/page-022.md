I have tried to use the kind of diction as one would find in a book like O’ Connor’s _Phonetics_² which I considered to be quite useful both for easy understanding and for scholarly pursuits.

Another very important concept I kept in mind is what one might call the Concept of Deposit and Withdrawal in translation.³ The trouble with many a translation is that the native speaker puts down in an array of words in a Target Language what he knows perfectly well in his Original Language. When he reads his translation he does not merely read a piece in the target language; in fact he reads a multiple in piece for, he knows to depth the original piece and he might even father on the new text things which do not exist there for a target language user who does not know the original language. Such a translator, I might say has made a good deposit. But the user of this translation is not able to withdraw from this text. The translator must know that that he is able to see the text clearly in the translation is no guarantee that the user will also understand it clearly. I have constantly kept in mind this fact that the translation should be clear to the English reader who does not read the translation after reading the original in Tamil. Moreover, I am very well aware of the fact that today’s English prose is simple, direct effective and functional. We do not use words of disproportionate magnitude. But simplicity in language does not mean simplicity in execution. The biggest casualty in this attempt for simplicity of the twentieth century English prose is the right type of emphasis. I have been witnessing a battle between the norms of simplicity and the need for the right emphasis throughout in this attempt. The original itself was a great model. In several places Tolkāppiyam expresses almost in a flash of words an intricate grammatical set up.
