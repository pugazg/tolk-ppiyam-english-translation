Another pitfall this translator has avoided is using in transliteration profusely the very Tamil words in the body of the verses. This, I know, disturbs the English reader. This is a very useful exercise for the learner of the language but does not help in the same plain the linguist-researcher. While the Tamil reader reads the English letters of the alphabet and makes out the shape and string of equivalents because of his previous knowledge of Tamil, the English reader who has no easy familiarity with Tamil finds it a great hurdle and so this translation avoids the transliterated Tamil words and uses them only when the very word or morpheme is kept in discussion. This seems to be a great advantage. When Ilakkuvanār said after Tolkāppiyam:

> The nouns are of two kinds which are  
> Said to be Uyarthiṇai and Aḵṟiṇai[^1]

probably this great Tamil savant read his Tamil in his English but the English reader is not pleased. A simpler illustration can make it even clearer. Dr. D. Albert says Tolkāppiyam: Morphology; he does not say Tolkappiyam: Collatikāram. This illustration is too simple to be effective but it illustrates the point.

Dr. D. Albert makes use of a very helpful aspect of the English language. As he mentions in the introduction the English language is rhythmic and musical in its very prose form. He explains that it is a regular beat language and so no one need to quarrel saying that his translation is not in verse form. English prose is English verse.

Kamil Zvelebil’s translation has appeared upto Morphology: Chapter 2 - On case, in the Journal of Tamil Studies. The erudite scholarship of this great Linguist is marked in every line of his translation. His concepts and terminology are based more on Linguistics, true to his scholarship, than on Applied Linguistics or Higher Grammar.
